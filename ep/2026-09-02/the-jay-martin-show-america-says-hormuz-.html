America Says Hormuz Wont Matter in 2 Years Heres the Problem

Host: Jay Martin · Solo episode · Listen at source ↗

Overview

Jay Martin analyzes the US government's claim, made by Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent and Secretary of State Marco Rubio, that the Strait of Hormuz will become "irrelevant" within two years, despite its current closure and critical role in global energy trade. Martin conducts an arithmetic assessment of existing and planned alternative oil and gas routes, concluding that the strait will remain highly relevant, though its closure may become less destructive due to new infrastructure. He emphasizes the importance of understanding the difference between reducing Iran's leverage and rendering the strait irrelevant.

Key discussion points

  • The Strait of Hormuz, which has been closed for six months, previously handled 20 million barrels of oil per day (a quarter of global seaborne oil) and nearly 20% of the world's LNG trade. Iran's actions, without sinking a single ship, caused over 90% of traffic to stop.
  • US Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent and Secretary of State Marco Rubio have publicly stated that the Strait of Hormuz will become "irrelevant" in two years, projecting a permanent shift in global energy routes.
  • Jay Martin's analysis of existing and planned oil bypass routes reveals that only 3.2 to 3.7 million barrels per day of spare capacity are available today (Saudi Arabia's East-West pipeline and UAE's ADCOF). A near-term UAE expansion could add another 1.8 million barrels per day by early to mid-2027, bringing the total to 5 to 5.5 million barrels per day.
  • No existing or planned alternative routes within the two-year window can transport natural gas (LNG) out of the Persian Gulf, leaving Qatar's and UAE's gas exports entirely dependent on the Strait of Hormuz.
  • Longer-term, within five years, combined Saudi and Emirati systems could potentially carry 12-13 million barrels per day, but only 9-10 million of that would be genuinely new capacity for oil previously crossing Hormuz, still leaving roughly half of the 20 million barrels without an alternative.
  • Martin highlights two critical rules for evaluating pipeline projects: 1) "the port matters as much as the pipe" (port loading capacity is the actual limit), and 2) "count how many governments have to agree on a pipeline" (every number above one adds risk).
  • Iraq's three potential pipeline solutions (Iraqi-Turkish, Kirkuk-Banias through Syria, and Iraq-Aqaba through Jordan) face significant political, financing, and security hurdles, with no concrete plans or construction expected within the two-year timeframe. The Aqaba route also introduces a new choke point at the Bab al-Mandeb Strait.
  • Martin concludes that while the Strait of Hormuz will not become irrelevant, the new infrastructure aims to reduce Iran's leverage by making a closure less destructive, allowing producers to continue exporting and buyers to receive some supply.

Market & macro takeaways

  • Jay Martin views the US government's claim of Hormuz irrelevance as a strategic attempt to talk down oil prices and reduce Iran's leverage, rather than a realistic forecast.
  • The ongoing closure of the Strait of Hormuz and the lack of immediate, viable alternatives for a significant portion of oil and all gas exports will maintain an "increased risk premium" on global energy prices.
  • This risk premium is expected to contribute to inflation numbers globally, impacting fuel-importing economies.
  • Investment in energy infrastructure, particularly within single-country control (like Saudi Arabia and UAE), is likely to continue and prove resilient post-crisis, driven by the need to reduce geopolitical choke point risks.
