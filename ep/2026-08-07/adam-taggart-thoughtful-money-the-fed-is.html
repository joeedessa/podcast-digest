Host: Adam Taggart · Guest: Dr. Judy Shelton, Economist, Author, Senior Fellow at the Independent Institute

Overview

This episode discusses the Federal Reserve's challenges under new Chairman Kevin Warsh, particularly regarding the growing national debt, persistent inflation, and external pressures like rising oil prices and foreign selling of US Treasuries. Judy Shelton and Thomas Hoenig both express concerns about the Fed's ability to navigate these issues without addressing underlying fiscal problems, with Hoenig emphasizing the "impossible problem" of the $2 trillion annual debt. Shelton praises Warsh's initial hawkish stance and task force approach, while Hoenig highlights the unavoidable conflict between the Fed's inflation target and the need to monetize debt.

Key discussion points

  • Kevin Warsh's leadership: Judy Shelton is "very favorably impressed" with Kevin Warsh's leadership, approving of his task force approach and his aim to let free market forces determine the cost of capital. Thomas Hoenig, who served with Warsh on the FOMC, believes Warsh "knows what to do" but faces difficult circumstances due to the growing national debt.
  • Fed independence vs. political considerations: Judy Shelton notes that while Warsh believes in monetary policy independence, political considerations, such as the Treasury's encouragement for Japan to use the FEMA facility, complicate the Fed's role. She expresses concern about "certain members trying to use say the the voices of of journalists at in at major newspapers to be their own fed whisperer."
  • US debt and foreign buyers: Thomas Hoenig states the US has a "big problem" with its debt, which puts pressure on the Fed to monetize it, otherwise yields will rise. He is "very concerned" about swap arrangements, viewing them as the Treasury looking to the Fed to fund Japan's purchases of US debt. Judy Shelton notes Japan is the largest holder of US debt, with "close to 1.2 trillion."
  • Safe haven assets: Thomas Hoenig believes the US dollar is still the safe haven, but questions "how many dollars do you want eventually." Judy Shelton points out that gold has "displaced the US dollar as the number one reserve asset held by major central banks around the world."
  • Solutions for the debt problem: Thomas Hoenig outlines three choices: continue monetizing debt (unsustainable), the Fed refusing to monetize (a "Volcker moment" with painful rate hikes), or Congress addressing spending and deficits, leading to stronger growth as seen post-WWII and in the mid-90s. Judy Shelton agrees that "productive economic growth" is the only way out, but fiscal overspending by Congress is a major impediment.
  • Gold standard and monetary governors: Thomas Hoenig suggests that a return to a gold standard "may by necessity" occur, as "all fiat systems historically have fallen apart." Judy Shelton, an advocate for tying the dollar to gold, cites Alan Greenspan's view that "unless there is some kind of governor on the issuance... there will be a tendency to overissue."
  • Fed's role in wealth inequality: Judy Shelton states the Fed "clearly has exacerbated the the wealth and income gap," leading to people feeling the "system's rigged." Thomas Hoenig agrees, noting that asset inflation, while CPI was low, created "arbitrary kick[s] from inflation from bad monetary policy."
  • Inflation target: Thomas Hoenig states he has "said that for decades" that the correct inflation target for the Fed should be 0%, believing "mild deflation is no no more worse than mild inflation." Judy Shelton agrees, stating "mild deflation. It's not only a good thing, but it's a natural order of things as technology increases the the standard of living at a lower price."
  • Fed's balance sheet and MBS: Both Judy Shelton and Thomas Hoenig agree that the Fed's initial foray into holding mortgage-backed securities (MBS) was a "mistake" and "inexplicable and and inexcusable." Hoenig thinks they should get rid of them, but slowly due to fear of disruption. Shelton notes the Fed has "800 billion in capital losses on on their treasuries and MBS."
