Host: Ed D'Agostino · Guest: Jason Hsu, Rayliant ETFs · Listen at source ↗

Overview

This episode features a discussion with Jason Hsu and Olivier Garret following a seven-day tour of mainland China. Ed D'Agostino, Jason Hsu, and Olivier Garret discuss the rapid development of China's tech sector, the investment implications for US investors, and common misconceptions about the Chinese economy. Hsu argues that US investors should consider allocating capital to China's tech sector due to its innovation, scale, and attractive valuations, particularly in hardware and manufacturing.

Key discussion points

  • Jason Hsu argues that most US investors operate from an outdated and biased image of China from 20 years ago, emphasizing the need to experience the country firsthand to understand its rapid reinvention.
  • Jason Hsu and Olivier Garret highlight that China's success is built on learning to respect, protect, and safeguard property rights for both domestic and international investors, a lesson learned from other successful Asian economies.
  • Olivier Garret and Jason Hsu criticize the US for "weaponizing the dollar" and tech, arguing that this policy accelerates the loss of dollar dominance and forces China to accelerate its development of critical tech for independence.
  • Olivier Garret notes the "breathtaking" focus on innovation in China, citing BYD's 70,000 patent applications as an example of the scale of effort to make a leap forward in technology.
  • Jason Hsu reframes the idea of China "stealing" Western technology, explaining that technology transfer is a historical developmental path, with the US willingly sharing technology to leverage cheaper manufacturing, which has now led to China's advanced engineering capabilities.
  • Jason Hsu explains that China's government investment model is decentralized, operating like venture capitalists who invest in businesses for good returns on capital, requiring co-investment from the private sector, and firing fund managers who don't produce returns.
  • Jason Hsu observes that China's market exhibits "textbook capitalism" with fierce competition that competes away corporate profit, leading to consumer surplus, in contrast to the US which incubates monopolies to capture profits.
  • Jason Hsu notes that China is now implementing industry policies to encourage consolidation to reduce perfect competition, allowing Chinese firms to capture more margin from distributors like Walmart and Amazon.
  • Jason Hsu discusses China's real estate "crisis" as a necessary bubble-pricking without the disastrous collapse seen in Japan, allowing the economy to transition and households to repair balance sheets, supported by strong exports.
  • Jason Hsu and Olivier Garret view US-China relations as a "G2 dynamic" where both nations are interdependent through trade, making a "divorce" too costly for either side, leading to tension but not a break.
  • Jason Hsu frames the core competition between the US and China as a dynamic between a distributor (US) and a manufacturer (China), with a potential shift where manufacturers capture more value due to technological edge and national policy.
  • Olivier Garret highlights the value proposition of China for investors, noting that Chinese companies represent a small percentage of global market cap despite producing 30% of world output, offering first-class companies at "bargain prices."
  • Jason Hsu predicts a "decade" of massive infrastructure build-out for AI deployment, similar to the post-tech bubble period (2001-2009) where hardware makers in emerging Asia outperformed NASDAQ, suggesting a similar opportunity for Chinese hardware manufacturers.
  • Jason Hsu describes the CNQQ ETF as a "smarter way to index" China, focusing on small- to mid-sized, hard-to-access mainland China-listed companies that are driving the next phase of growth in innovative technology and AI, rather than mature, large-cap internet names.
