We Asked T Rowes 8 Billion Tech Manager Why We Are in 1998 And Why Software Is i

Host: Jack Forehand, Kai Woo · Guest: Dom Rizzle, Portfolio Manager, T. Rowe Price Global Technology Equity Strategy and Technology ETF · Listen at source ↗

Overview

Dom Rizzle discusses the current AI-driven technology market, drawing parallels to the 1998 tech correction but highlighting fundamental differences in revenue growth. He argues that the current capital cycle for AI infrastructure is only halfway through, with hyperscalers poised for accelerating capex spend due to strong ROICs. Rizzle also explores the potential for frontier AI labs to capture significant economic value, the financing of the AI buildout, and the structural capital intensiveness of AI compared to traditional software.

Key discussion points

  • Dom Rizzle compares the recent market action in tech stocks to the 1998 sell-off, noting similarities in high momentum factor volatility and geopolitical shocks, but a key fundamental difference in semiconductor revenue growth (64% increase in 2026 vs. 8% decline in 1998).
  • Rizzle believes the AI capital cycle is only halfway through, with hyperscaler capex spend expected to accelerate beyond the 75% growth seen in 2026, potentially reaching $1.5-$1.6 trillion, even with moderate memory pricing.
  • He highlights strong ROICs for hyperscalers like Amazon (AWS growth accelerating to 37% at 50% incremental operating margins), Microsoft (Azure growing 43%, guiding to 45%), and Google (GCP growing 82% at 50% incremental operating margins), supported by a Morgan Stanley analysis of 30% ROIC for the broader hyperscaler universe.
  • Rizzle identifies a potential pitfall in the AI ecosystem: the rapid growth of AI labs like OpenAI and Anthropic, which could eventually aggregate frontier intelligence and put pressure on hyperscalers, with OpenAI already representing 25% of Azure revenue.
  • He argues that while 80% of tokens may be open source or N-1 models, the vast majority of economic value will accrue to frontier models due to their ability to orchestrate other models and drive productivity gains, particularly in coding.
  • Rizzle suggests that traditional application software is "in trouble" because the application interface is shifting from humans to agents, and AI-native software design is fundamentally different, with established systems of record becoming "dumb data pipes."
  • He believes semiconductors will always be cyclical but are currently in a "great upcycle" driven by AI's structural capital intensiveness and the scaling laws, which show no signs of ending.
  • Rizzle acknowledges that AI will displace some jobs but leans towards an "age of abundance" camp, where productivity gains lead to revenue and GDP growth, ultimately creating more jobs, albeit with high churn in the economy.
  • He emphasizes the importance of understanding reflexive cycles in investing, citing George Soros's "Alchemy of Finance," and advises investors to "buy accelerating fundamentals."
