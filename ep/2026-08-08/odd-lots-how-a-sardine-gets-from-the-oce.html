How a Sardine Gets From the Ocean to a Can

Host: Tracy Alloway, Joe Weisenthal · Guest: Becca Milstein, Founder and CEO of Fish Wife

Overview

This episode explores the global supply chain of tinned fish, focusing on the recent sardine shortage and its implications. Becca Milstein, founder of Fish Wife, discusses the challenges and opportunities in building a premium tinned fish brand, navigating complex international supply chains, and the impact of climate change and market dynamics on fish stocks and pricing. Tracy Alloway and Joe Weisenthal highlight the intersection of global supply chains, climate issues, and consumer demand in the niche but growing premium tinned fish market.

Key discussion points

  • Becca Milstein founded Fish Wife to create a premium tinned fish product for the American market, inspired by European consumption habits, countering the existing commodity perception of canned fish.
  • The supply chain for Fish Wife's Cantabrian anchovies involves local fishermen in Santonia, Spain, using the Persene method, immediate icing, an anchovy auction, and a 12-36 month salt-curing process in multi-generational family-owned canneries.
  • Becca Milstein noted that securing co-manufacturers in Europe was initially easier than in the US because the canned fish industry was flat or declining, and European canneries had slack capacity and saw the US as an attractive, affluent market for premium products.
  • Fish Wife's initial supply chain involved tiny micro-canneries in Oregon and Washington State with no minimum order quantity (MOQ), allowing for brand building despite early inventory challenges and unexpected stock-outs.
  • The Moroccan sardine export ban, imposed due to overfishing and a lack of plankton, specifically targets frozen sardine exports, allowing Morocco to preserve raw material for its own canned sardine industry.
  • Fish Wife was not directly impacted by the Moroccan ban in terms of raw material supply because they source from MSC-certified Cornish and Iberian sardine fisheries, but they experienced a 2-5x surge in sardine sales due to market scarcity.
  • Becca Milstein attributes the sardine shortage theories to climate change, including warming mid-Atlantic waters causing sardine migration to colder, deeper, or more distant areas, and changes in the food web leading to more predators or less nutrient-dense plankton.
  • Becca Milstein states that overfishing is "not the main culprit" in the current sardine situation, citing the Monterey Bay Aquarium Seafood Watch's yellow rating for the fishery, indicating good regulation and strong enforcement.
  • Becca Milstein explains that "responsible fisheries management in practice" involves capping or shutting down fisheries when fish populations decline, allowing them to repopulate, with many examples of full recovery within 3-5 years.
  • Fish Wife's business is only about 20% DTC, with the majority of volume coming from retail, indicating a shift from the "golden age of Instagram" DTC playbook where brands focused on cutting costs and advertising heavily online.
  • Becca Milstein notes that brand collaborations for Fish Wife are "so the former," meaning they are in-depth, months-long projects with no money exchanged, focusing on genuine product integration rather than pay-to-play arrangements.
  • Fish Wife uses an affiliate program for influencers, where they earn commission on sales, rather than paying high flat fees, as the latter can be "astronomical rates" of $30,000-$40,000 for influencers with 800,000 followers.
  • Becca Milstein states that tariffs on aluminum packaging have not been the biggest hit to Fish Wife; instead, a 15% tariff on products imported from Europe, implemented in April 2025, has cost the business "millions and millions of dollars."
  • Becca Milstein explains that Fish Wife uses farmed salmon for its smoked salmon products due to the difficulty of scaling a wild Alaskan salmon supply chain, the year-round production of farmed salmon, and its more manageable and less volatile cash outlay compared to seasonal wild-caught fishing.

Market & macro takeaways

  • Becca Milstein's view: The premiumization of commodity products, particularly in food, is a viable business model, as consumers are willing to pay for higher quality, especially when traditional DTC models face increased advertising costs.
  • Becca Milstein's view: Global supply chains for niche products like tinned fish are highly susceptible to climate change impacts (e.g., warming waters, plankton availability) and geopolitical decisions (e.g., export bans), leading to rapid shifts in demand and pricing.
  • Becca Milstein's view: Responsible fisheries management, including temporary shutdowns or export bans, can lead to long-term sustainability and repopulation of fish stocks, which is a positive for the industry despite short-term supply disruptions.
  • Becca Milstein's view: The "trading up" phenomenon in food inflation is real, where consumers facing higher prices for commodity items may opt for premium alternatives, impacting demand across different market segments.
