Overview

Steve Eisman interviews Adrian Wooldridge, co-author with Alan Greenspan, on the economic history of the United States, coinciding with the 250th anniversary of the Declaration of Independence. They discuss key historical economic debates, the impact of major industrial innovations like railroads, and the causes of historical financial crises, including the Great Depression and the Great Financial Crisis. Wooldridge shares insights into Greenspan's views and their collaborative process, while Eisman offers his own critiques on Greenspan's legacy regarding the GFC.

Key discussion points

  • Adrian Wooldridge's career path involved starting as an Oxford academic, drifting into journalism at The Economist for 32 years, covering British politics, management theory, and then moving to Bloomberg to write about global business.
  • The collaboration with Alan Greenspan for "Capitalism in America" came about through their shared editor, Scott Moyers. Wooldridge underwent an "interviewing process" with Greenspan, who was intrigued by Wooldridge's location in Edinburgh, associated with Adam Smith, and his book "The Company."
  • The first major economic debate in the US was between Alexander Hamilton and Thomas Jefferson. Jefferson advocated for dispersed power and an agricultural, rural economy to prevent tyranny, while Hamilton envisioned a modern world of cities, division of labor, productivity, and a powerful central government with a stable banking system.
  • By the time of the Civil War, Hamilton's vision had largely won, aligning with the powerful forces of industry, cities, and the demand for a stable banking system. Hamilton was also an early proponent of the "infant industry" argument for tariffs to protect nascent American industries from British dominance.
  • The railroad was identified as a critical innovation after the Civil War, alongside steel and oil, transforming the US from a Jeffersonian country to an industrial one. Railroads "shrank the country," creating a national economy and necessitating corporate structures and coordinated time zones.
  • Wooldridge draws parallels between the railroad boom and the current AI boom, noting that both involved massive building programs anticipating demand, significant speculative investment, and subsequent bankruptcies. He states that "very few people invested who invested in the railroads actually made much money out of the railroads."
  • The US experienced frequent crashes and depressions before the Great Depression (1819, 1837, 1857, 1873, 1884, 1893, 1896, 1907). Alan Greenspan viewed these as part of capitalism's "creative destruction," displacing capital to more productive uses, with the gold standard providing discipline.
  • The "robber barons" of the post-Civil War era, like Carnegie and Rockefeller, prospered by exploiting economies of scale and technological innovation, driving down unit costs of essential goods like steel and oil, rather than through price gouging. This was facilitated by the perfection of the limited liability joint stock company.
  • Regarding the Great Depression, Alan Greenspan believed FDR "made the depression worse" and was not a fan of the New Deal. Wooldridge, however, suggests FDR's actions, particularly in exuding confidence and stopping bank runs, were important, and notes the 6-month gap between Hoover's election loss and FDR's inauguration exacerbated the crisis.
  • On the Great Financial Crisis, Alan Greenspan, who wrote that section of the book, primarily blamed "poor regulation of the housing market" and the "cross-party consensus" to increase homeownership, particularly among ethnic minorities, and the governance of Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac.
  • Steve Eisman explicitly disagreed with Greenspan's GFC assessment, arguing that the primary cause was the "excess leverage that Greenspan allowed into the banking system" between 1997 and 2007, which more than tripled, enabled by the system of risk-weighted assets.
  • The major lessons from US economic history are the power of combining well-managed corporations with entrepreneurial genius to invent the future. America's culture, appetite for risk, and the societal expression of genius through building great companies contribute to its unique dynamism.

Market & macro takeaways

  • Adrian Wooldridge views the current AI boom as having striking parallels to the railroad boom, suggesting that while it may be good for the country and a few top players, many smaller investors could lose money due to speculative bubbles and over-investment.
  • Steve Eisman's critique of Alan Greenspan's role in the GFC highlights the dangers of excessive leverage in the banking system, particularly when enabled by risk-weighted asset models and over-reliance on banks to manage their own risk. This implies a need for vigilance regarding financial sector leverage.
  • Adrian Wooldridge expresses optimism for America's future due to its unique capacity to combine well-managed corporations with entrepreneurial genius, its risk-taking culture, and its societal drive to build great companies, which he sees as unparalleled globally.
