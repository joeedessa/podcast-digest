Who Killed The Random Walk Victor Haghani on Momentum Passive Investing and LTCM

Host: Jack Farley · Guest: Victor Haghani, Founding Partner of Long-Term Capital Management and Elm Wealth

Overview

Victor Haghani discusses his paper "Who Killed The Random Walk," which challenges the efficient market hypothesis by explaining stock market puzzles through a multi-agent model. He argues that market dynamics are primarily driven by "extrapolators" (return chasers), "static asset allocators," and corporate supply/demand, rather than fundamental earnings. Haghani also differentiates between passive stock investing (which he supports) and passive asset allocation (which he views as problematic).

Key discussion points

  • LTCM Lessons: Victor Haghani identifies key lessons from Long-Term Capital Management's failure, including the importance of personal risk-taking, skin in the game, and the potential pitfalls of running a leveraged pool of standalone capital. He suggests relative value trading might be better within larger institutions and highlights the need for tight stop losses, which LTCM lacked.
  • Challenging the Random Walk: Haghani's paper, "Who Killed The Random Walk," argues that the stock market does not follow a classical random walk. It aims to explain puzzles like excess volatility, clustered volatility, trends (momentum), and booms and busts through a heterogeneous belief model.
  • Investor Types: The model includes three main investor types: "value investors" who focus on long-term earnings and dividends, "static investors" (passive asset allocators) who maintain fixed allocations (e.g., 60/40), and "extrapolators" (return chasers) who base future return expectations on recent history.
  • Passive Asset Allocation vs. Passive Stock Investing: Haghani distinguishes between passive asset allocation (e.g., sticking to 60/40 regardless of market conditions), which he finds problematic and illogical, and passive stock investing (e.g., investing in market-cap-weighted index funds), which he supports as a valid way to gain stock exposure.
  • Momentum vs. Extrapolation: Haghani explains that momentum investing, characterized by binary, fixed-amount exposure changes based on positive or negative momentum, has historically performed well. In contrast, extrapolators (return chasers) make continuous, slow-moving adjustments to their allocations based on past returns, which tends to perform poorly.
  • Market Inelasticity: Haghani believes the stock market is not perfectly elastic, meaning large supply/demand changes (like corporate buybacks or IPOs) can significantly move prices. He estimates a trillion dollars in buybacks could move the market up by 3-4%.
  • US Equity Outlook: Haghani and other market observers forecast a long-term expected return for US equities of around 6%, only 1% more than 30-year Treasuries. He attributes this low risk premium to static asset allocators, extrapolators, and corporate buybacks, rather than strong fundamental earnings growth.
  • Earnings Growth and Valuation: Haghani argues that current high earnings growth rates (e.g., 24% for S&P operating earnings) are not sustainable long-term and should be viewed through cyclically adjusted earnings. He believes that if 25% earnings growth continued for 3-4 years, corporate earnings would become an unrealistic 50% of GDP.
  • Drivers of Aggregate Market Level: Haghani contends that the overall market level is less determined by fundamental investors and earnings, and more by extrapolators, static asset allocators, and corporate supply/demand (issuance, buybacks), as well as changes in savings flows into the market.
  • Elm Wealth's Investment Approach: Elm Wealth employs a dynamic asset allocation strategy based on a "five-star screen" for risky assets, focusing on assets with a verifiable risk premium, broad diversification, liquidity, transparency, low fees, and tax efficiency. This leads them to low-cost, broad-market index funds and real estate investment trusts.
  • Current Allocation at Elm Wealth: Elm Wealth is currently underweight US equities (less than 30% vs. a 40% baseline) due to low expected long-term returns (around 1% risk premium) despite positive momentum. They are overweight non-US equities due to better expected returns and positive momentum. Overall, they are slightly underweight equities and overweight fixed income (mostly Treasury bills).

  • Victor Haghani believes current US equity valuations are "very elevated" and the overall market level "looks kind of ridiculous right now."
  • Victor Haghani suggests non-US equities offer "healthier long-term returns relative to safe assets" compared to US equities.
  • Victor Haghani attributes high US stock prices to corporate buybacks (trillion dollars annually, estimated 3-4% market uplift), low IPO calendar, capital-light businesses, extrapolators, and static asset allocators.
  • Victor Haghani predicts that if IPO issuance, secondary issuance, insider sales (e.g., SpaceX), and a reduction in buybacks create a "delta of 1 to two trillion dollars," it will "weigh on the market."
