Victor Haghani on Death of Random Walk and Passive Buybacks and LTCM

Host: Jack Farley · Guest: Victor Haghani, Founding Partner of Long-Term Capital Management and Co-author of "The Missing Billionaires" and an upcoming book, Elm Wealth · Listen at source ↗

Overview

Victor Haghani discusses his research on the "Death of the Random Walk" theory, arguing that traditional financial models fail to explain market anomalies like excess volatility, trends, and booms/busts. He introduces a multi-agent model featuring value investors, static asset allocators, and extrapolators to explain these phenomena. Haghani also critiques passive asset allocation, distinguishing it from passive stock investing, and outlines Elm Wealth's dynamic asset allocation strategy.

Key discussion points

  • Victor Haghani's paper, "Who Killed the Random Walk? Why Extrapolators Explain Booms and Busts and Other Stock Market Puzzles and Anomalies," challenges the random walk theory by explaining market puzzles through a multi-agent model.
  • The model includes three investor types: value investors (focused on long-term earnings), static investors (maintaining fixed asset allocations), and extrapolators/return chasers (basing future expectations on recent returns).
  • Haghani argues that passive asset allocation (e.g., fixed 60-40 portfolios) is problematic and contributes to market booms and busts, while passive stock investing (market-cap weighted index funds) is generally beneficial.
  • Haghani believes the overall U.S. stock market is "ridiculous" in its valuation, with consensus long-term expected returns for U.S. equities around 6%, only 1% more than 30-year Treasuries.
  • He attributes elevated U.S. stock prices to extrapolators, corporate buybacks (over a trillion dollars annually), low IPO issuance, and static asset allocators who do not adjust their holdings based on changing valuations.
  • Haghani distinguishes between momentum investors (binary, discrete changes in exposure) and extrapolators/return chasers (continuous, slow-moving changes in exposure based on past returns), noting that momentum strategies tend to perform well while return chasing strategies perform poorly.
  • He argues that earnings growth, particularly short-term, is not the primary driver of aggregate market dynamics, but rather extrapolator behavior, static asset allocation, and supply/demand changes (issuance, buybacks, savings flows).
  • Haghani explains that when adjusting for share buybacks and retained earnings, core earnings growth over the past 30 years has been modest, despite significant technological advancements.
  • Elm Wealth's investment approach is a low-cost, rules-based dynamic asset allocation strategy based on long-term expected risk premiums, risk metrics (with momentum as a proxy), and a "five-star screen" for risky assets.
  • The "five-star screen" requires risky assets to have a systematic risk premium, an estimable risk premium, offer pure systematic risk (broadly diversified), be liquid and transparent, and have low fees and tax efficiency.
  • Applying this strategy, Elm Wealth is currently underweight U.S. equities due to low expected returns relative to safe assets (around 1% risk premium) and overweight non-U.S. equities due to better valuations and positive momentum.
  • Haghani suggests that the best investment approach combines value and momentum, as proposed by Cliff Asness, where long-term cash flows are considered alongside momentum as a risk signal.

Market & macro takeaways

  • Victor Haghani views the overall U.S. stock market as overvalued, with a low expected long-term return of 6% and a risk premium of only 1% over safe assets. This is driven by extrapolators, corporate buybacks, and static asset allocators, rather than fundamental earnings.
  • He believes non-U.S. equities offer healthier long-term returns compared to U.S. equities, leading Elm Wealth to be underweight U.S. and overweight non-U.S. equities.
  • Haghani suggests that the market is not perfectly elastic, and corporate buybacks (trillions annually) and other supply/demand changes have a significant price impact, potentially moving the market up by 3-4%.
  • He argues that short-term earnings growth, even if strong, should be viewed in the context of cyclically adjusted earnings, as sustained high growth rates are unrealistic and competition erodes margins.
  • Haghani's firm, Elm Wealth, implements a dynamic asset allocation strategy that adjusts exposure to asset classes based on their long-term expected risk premium and risk (using momentum as a proxy), currently favoring non-U.S. equities and treasury bills.
