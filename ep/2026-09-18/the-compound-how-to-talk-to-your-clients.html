How to Talk To Your Clients About Ray Dalio Animal Spirits 482

Host: Michael Batnick, Ben Carlson · Guest: Colin Roche, Discipline Funds · Listen at source ↗

Overview

Michael Batnick and Ben Carlson discuss the pervasive influence of Ray Dalio's pessimistic macro views on clients and the broader market. They invite Colin Roche to provide a nuanced perspective on government debt, inflation, and fixed income, arguing that while risks exist, Dalio's dire predictions often overlook crucial distinctions and potential rewards, particularly in the current bond market. The discussion also touches on the societal and economic implications of AI, the role of policy errors in financial crises, and the changing dynamics of asset allocation.

Key discussion points

  • Michael Batnick notes the repetitive nature of market discourse and highlights recent "perfect internet discourse" around an Anthropic employee's resignation, citing a 10% or greater chance of civilization being wiped out by AI, and Ray Dalio's response to "slow down."
  • Ben Carlson observes the extreme sentiment surrounding AI, ranging from "useless technology" (Ed Zitron) to "destroy humanity" or "create a utopia with 20% economic growth," and expresses skepticism about these extreme outcomes.
  • Michael Batnick questions the incentive structure behind AI doomsaying, particularly how it could be a fundraising strategy for companies seeking multi-trillion dollar valuations, finding the cynical take less credible than taking the threats at face value.
  • Ben Carlson cites Derek Thompson's observation that warnings about AI risks have existed for years, but the recent resignation of a specific individual from Anthropic went viral, highlighting the mysterious nature of how narratives catch on.
  • Ben Carlson suggests that the next major financial crisis is more likely to stem from a policy error, such as the Fed or government overstepping, rather than an inherent economic downturn, drawing parallels to the 1873 Long Depression.
  • Michael Batnick notes that despite macro headwinds like rising mortgage rates (6% to 7%), 10-year Treasury yields (4% to 5%), and inflation (2.5% to 3.5%), the S&P 500 is up 12-17% on the year, indicating a disconnect or the market's predictive power.
  • Michael Batnick highlights a significant rotation within the market, noting that semiconductors, which were up 80% through July, are now almost in a 20% drawdown, while the overall stock market is only down about 4%.
  • Michael Batnick expresses frustration with Ray Dalio's public communication, particularly his analogy of the US government as a "big business" and his use of figures like "$240,000 per American household" for federal debt, which Michael finds irrelevant and fear-mongering.
  • Colin Roche distinguishes the US from countries that "go broke" by emphasizing that the US can print its own currency and does not borrow in a foreign currency, making default an unlikely risk.
  • Colin Roche argues that the primary risk from government spending is inflation, not default, and that inflation impacts different assets differently.
  • Colin Roche points out that a 10-year TIPS yield at 2.6% is "really attractive," suggesting that if inflation averages 5% over 10 years, clients could achieve a 7.6% nominal return on a "risk-free" asset.
  • Colin Roche states that a T-bill yielding 4.6% is a "no-brainer" for short-term liabilities, offering an inflation-adjusted return with zero default probability.
  • Colin Roche suggests that hedge fund managers like Dalio, Paul Tudor Jones, and Stanley Druckenmiller often have a pessimistic disposition focused on risk management, whereas financial advisors helping households tend to be more optimistic, viewing equities as the best long-term inflation hedge.
  • Colin Roche explains that the risk-reward for bonds has substantially changed since 2019, when a 10-year Treasury yielding 1% had low risk-reward, compared to today's 5% yield with the same modified duration, offering a much better cushion.
  • Colin Roche advocates for time-horizon-based investing, particularly for the 0-5 year time horizon in bonds, where the yield cushion can offset interest rate risk.
  • Ben Carlson questions the effectiveness of Fed rate hikes in slowing the economy when AI buildout by hyperscalers continues to drive investment, suggesting that slowing the AI buildout might be the only way to truly slow the economy.
  • Colin Roche believes that AI is "incredibly disinflationary in the long run," as it could lead to collapsing unit labor costs and numerous deflationary knock-on effects, potentially requiring greater government involvement to offset job losses and wage collapses.
  • Colin Roche identifies hyperscaler investment as "probably the biggest risk" to the economy, noting that data centers have been offsetting declining residential and corporate investment, and a pullback in AI investment could significantly impact GDP.
  • Colin Roche asserts that the federal debt rising to $55-60 trillion over the next decade is likely, but the primary risk remains inflation, not the US going bankrupt, given the US economy's size ($450 trillion in total financial assets) and its ability to tax the wealthiest society.
  • Colin Roche dismisses the idea of a "buyer strike" against US Treasuries, arguing that global alternatives are "all worse," and the US remains the "cleanest dirty shirt in the closet" with 60% of foreign currency reserves.
