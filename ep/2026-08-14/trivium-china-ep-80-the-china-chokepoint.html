Ep 80 - The China chokepoints nobodys watching

Host: Andrew Polk · Guest: Gerard DePippa, Director of Global Macro, Eurasia Group · Listen at source ↗

Overview

This episode discusses China's evolving economic coercion playbook, moving beyond critical minerals to intermediate goods. Gerard DePippa argues that China's industrial policy is expanding its market share in intermediate goods, creating new choke points that Western governments are not adequately planning for. Dinny McMahon and Corey Combs add that while China's initial focus on intermediate goods was defensive, its success has created weaponization opportunities, and the West's reactive industrial policy struggles to keep pace.

Key discussion points

  • Gerard DePippa states that while critical minerals remain Beijing's preferred retaliatory tool, other choke points are developing, particularly in intermediate goods, implying China's coercive leverage is growing in ways Western governments are not planning for.
  • Gerard DePippa explains that Beijing leans on licensing for export controls rather than outright bans to collect supply chain information, detect stockpiling, and maintain a short leash on companies, allowing for quick pressure escalation.
  • Corey Combs highlights that there is a systematic effort in China, from city council level to central planners, to map and understand China's vulnerabilities, driving both state and private sector investment in overseas mining and processing to ensure mineral resiliency.
  • Dinny McMahon notes that China's focus on overseas investments for securing critical materials is not new, but its use of export controls to protect technology, particularly for magnets, is a more recent development.
  • Gerard DePippa discusses China's version of the foreign direct product rule, shelved as part of the Busan truce, which could allow China to restrict goods containing any non-negligible amount of Chinese rare earth content, even if processed elsewhere.
  • Corey Combs distinguishes between the non-negotiable existence of a licensing requirement for export controls and the flexible escalation options, such as prohibitions or extraterritorial application, which Beijing can deploy or retract.
  • Gerard DePippa argues that intermediate goods are better weapons than final goods because they can disrupt production in a target economy, potentially across multiple industries, with less political pushback and lower economic cost to Chinese firms.
  • Dinny McMahon suggests that China's aggressive expansion into intermediate goods, such as chemicals and pharmaceutical precursors, poses a threat to developed economies by hollowing out their own industries, making China the "OPEC of intermediate goods."
  • Gerard DePippa identifies five broad criteria Beijing uses to assess a good's weaponization potential: harm to target economy, replaceability, cost to Chinese industry, enforcement capacity, and reputational risk (e.g., pharmaceutical APIs are politically suboptimal).
  • Corey Combs suggests that proximate risks include expanding existing controls further down the production chain for materials like super hard materials (e.g., tungsten carbide, carbon boron nitride, diamond windows) and certain types of silicon carbide.
  • Gerard DePippa states that the U.S. industrial policy apparatus is less intricate and developed than China's, making it more reactive and less capable of front-running Chinese industrial policy, especially for lower-value-added intermediate goods.
  • Dinny McMahon posits that China's overreach might be at a macro level, specifically its full-throated defense of its trade surplus and the current free trade regime, which increasingly leaves other countries feeling they are not benefiting as intended.
  • Gerard DePippa concludes that China is trying to teach the West that it does not have unilateral freedom of action in imposing offensive measures, leading to a new equilibrium where both sides have demonstrated weapons and must be more careful in policy.
