We Asked a China Expert Who Wins If the US Slows AI Down And Why the SP 500 is O

Host: Justin Carbonneau, Jack Forehand · Guest: Jason Hsu, Founder and Chief Investment Officer at Rayliant Global Advisors · Listen at source ↗

Overview

Jason Hsu discusses the global AI race between the US and China, emphasizing China's rapid technological catch-up and energy advantages. He argues that the value in AI will shift from models to hardware and energy providers, and later to application integrators. Hsu also highlights the unique opportunities for factor investing in China's retail-dominated market and warns passive US investors about the current concentration risk in the S&P 500.

Key discussion points

  • China's AI development is now on par with the US in model development and potentially has an edge in real-world model implementation, having closed the gap from 20% behind to "a hair length away" or "less than 5%" in a year.
  • US attempts to regulate AI development will only stunt US firms while having no impact on China, as China will "simply leapfrog" any US slowdown. Global coordination between the US and China is necessary for AI security.
  • China's major AI model developers are its largest tech companies (Alibaba, Tencent, Baidu), mirroring the US's "Magnificent Seven," with the exception of DeepSeek, which originated as a high-frequency hedge fund.
  • China possesses a significant competitive advantage in energy and power transmission, with multi-sourced energy production (Russian, Middle Eastern, Omani, Central Asian, solar, nuclear) and an ultra-high efficiency grid, which is crucial for AI data centers.
  • In the long run, most of the value in AI will not be in the models themselves due to convergence and the rise of open-source alternatives. Instead, value will accrue to hardware providers, energy providers, and application integrators who build solutions around the cheapest models.
  • There will likely be a "good 10-year period where hardware is going to capture most of the rent" in the AI cycle, followed by application companies building custom solutions on top of "almost a free now kind of hardware infrastructure."
  • US restrictions on chip technology for China have encouraged China to become self-sufficient, rapidly closing the gap in chip capabilities, though they are not yet near Nvidia GPU or TSMC foundry quality.
  • China's stock market, being 80-90% retail-owned and half retail in trading volume, is a "fertile ground to create alpha" for factor investing due to abundant short-term overreaction, underreaction, and long-term mispricings.
  • The Chinese market is barbell-shaped, with vibrant privately-owned tech firms and "super boring state-owned enterprises" (banks, utilities, alcohol, tobacco) that offer "incredibly high dividend paying value stocks" and can serve as a "fixed income replacement portfolio."
  • US and China need each other macroeconomically: the US, as a welfare state with high government expenditure and inflation, needs China's deflationary, productive manufacturing exports, while China needs US dollars.
  • Current US AI-related capex involves an "arms race" where overspending by competitors leads to "increasingly lower return," but firms are "pulling future cash flow to today" by borrowing at great rates, not due to financial distress.
  • Passive investors in the S&P 500 are currently "actively choosing to bet the farm on one thing" (AI capex story) and are not truly diversified, as the market beta shifts over time (e.g., internet, real estate/finance, now AI).
  • Momentum is a "great factor" but is prone to crashes, which are "part of the game" and reset the trade. Value is a "great factor to run side by side with momentum" as they work on different cycles and are good diversifiers.
  • In the US, "super popular factors" like value and small cap have "done quite poorly... for like 30 years" and are likely "arbed out," but "stuff that aren't taught in the US" (e.g., momentum, quality growth-oriented factors) are "still reliable."
  • Machine learning and advanced computational techniques allow for the use of "hundreds of factors" in portfolio construction, preventing overfitting and enabling more sophisticated robustness checking.
  • Multi-factor portfolios often have fewer names than benchmarks but offer "more meaningful more diversified factor exposure" by giving proper weights to liquid, meaningful names and adjusting for factor effectiveness in the market.
  • Outperformance in multi-factor portfolios comes from a blend of "avoiding the bad stuff" (downward protection from unreasonably priced thematic stocks) and concentrating in "truly good quality growth oriented stock" with sustainable fundamentals.
  • Financial advisors should outsource investing and focus on helping clients "enjoy being wealthy" and "build meaning from their wealth," rather than stock-picking, where they lack an edge against top-tier professionals.

Market & macro takeaways

  • Jason Hsu views China's energy policy and infrastructure as a significant competitive advantage in the AI race, potentially more critical than technology itself.
  • He predicts a long-term shift in AI value capture from software models to hardware and energy, followed by application integrators.
  • Hsu suggests that the S&P 500, due to its current concentration in AI-related firms, is not truly diversified for passive investors and carries significant bubble risk.
  • He advocates for multi-factor investing, particularly combining momentum and value, as a way to achieve diversification and capture alpha, especially in inefficient markets like China.
  • Hsu believes that while traditional factors like value and small cap may be "arbed out" in the US, other factors like momentum and quality growth still offer reliable premiums.
