The Mining Exploration Boom Has Barely Begun Major Drilling

Host: Trevor Hall · Guest: Ryan Hanley, Director of Capital Markets, Major Drilling · Listen at source ↗

Overview

This episode features a discussion with Ryan Hanley of Major Drilling, focusing on the drilling industry's perspective on the mining exploration cycle. Hanley explains that the industry is in the early stages of a new cycle, driven by increased exploration budgets from senior miners and a resurgence in junior financing, with specialized drilling becoming increasingly important due to deeper and more remote deposits. He also highlights the challenges of labor and the evolving landscape of exploration financing, including the role of AI in target refinement.

Key discussion points

  • Ryan Hanley's career background includes 16 years in capital markets, starting in equity research covering mining companies and drillers, including Major Drilling (MDI) for 12 years, before joining Major Drilling as Director of Capital Markets in late August/Labor Day of 2024.
  • Major Drilling's business model is straightforward, with financials easier to understand than mining companies, but forecasting key drivers like margins and revenue is challenging as it depends on senior mining companies' budgets and junior financing.
  • Major Drilling is a lagging indicator in the mining cycle; its operating results typically trail exploration budgets of seniors and the financing window of juniors. The company is only now starting to see benefits from increased exploration.
  • Global exploration spending was estimated at $12.5 billion for calendar 2025, significantly below the 2012 peak of $21.5 billion (not inflation-adjusted), suggesting substantial room for growth in the current cycle.
  • Senior mining companies' exploration budgets for 2026 are up by an average of 30-35% year-over-year, with Barrick Gold's budget up 35% as an example, indicating a shift towards organic growth.
  • A new theme in this cycle is senior miners taking equity stakes in juniors, using them as an exploration tool to gain exposure to greenfield projects at relatively low risk, exemplified by Newmont's holdings.
  • The industry is seeing a lack of new large gold and copper discoveries, leading to a focus on exploration to replenish depleting reserves.
  • Major Drilling specializes in "specialized drilling" for complex projects in challenging conditions (e.g., Canadian Arctic, high-altitude South America) and deep-hole drilling, with records for deepest drill holes (e.g., 3,500-meter holes for Oys 1.0 at Windfall).
  • Labor is the biggest challenge for the drilling industry, with Major Drilling's utilization rate at 58% (max 75%) due to difficulty finding drillers. Compensation is good, with productivity bonuses, and the company focuses on training and safety.
  • The lag between junior financing and revenue for drillers is about three quarters (7-9 months). Junior revenue for Major Drilling has increased from 8% to 15% of total revenue over recent quarters, still far from the 35% peak of the last cycle.
  • Increased M&A activity (e.g., Orion acquired by Nico, Roxgold by SSR) is a positive tailwind for the junior market, as capital from successful exits is reinvested into new junior companies.
  • Seniors are now more willing to pay for quality drilling services, prioritizing efficiency and properly maintained rigs with trained crews over the cheapest option, a shift from trough times.
  • Geographically, North America (Canada and US) is the busiest market for Major Drilling, but new regions like Argentina and Guyana are emerging as attractive for exploration due to changing political landscapes and increased interest from seniors.
  • Saudi Arabia is identified as a potentially interesting new region for exploration, with the government aiming to diversify away from energy into critical metals, though establishing the regulatory framework may take time.
  • Well-run exploration companies are characterized by a good team with a strong track record, a good project in a good location, responsible management of G&A expenses (prioritizing exploration expenditures), and avoiding reliance on favorable geopolitical outcomes or commodity price increases.
  • AI and other technologies for target refinement are beneficial for drilling companies because they lead to more successful discoveries, which then result in significantly expanded drill programs (e.g., from 2 rigs to 20 after a discovery at OT in Mongolia).

Market & macro takeaways

  • Ryan Hanley views the mining exploration sector as being in the early innings of a new cycle, with significant upside potential given current exploration spending levels are still well below previous peaks when adjusted for inflation.
  • Senior miners are increasing exploration budgets (30-35% YoY for 2026) and increasingly using equity stakes in junior companies as a low-risk exploration tool.
  • M&A activity in the junior mining space is a positive catalyst, as capital from successful takeouts is typically reinvested into other junior exploration companies, creating a "snowball effect."
  • Labor shortages for drillers are a key bottleneck for the industry, impacting utilization rates despite available rigs.
  • Geographically, North America is currently the busiest, but South American countries like Argentina and Guyana, and potentially the Middle East (e.g., Saudi Arabia), are emerging as new areas of interest for exploration.
  • AI and technology are seen as beneficial for the drilling industry, as they help refine drill targets, leading to more successful discoveries and subsequently larger, more profitable drill programs.
