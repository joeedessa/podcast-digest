This Market Beating Hedge Fund Manager Explains Recent Tech Sector Rotation Jeff

Host: Max Wiethe · Guest: Jeff Keller, Founder and Portfolio Manager of Cape Light Partners · Listen at source ↗

Overview

Jeff Keller, founder of Cape Light Partners, discusses the recent rotation within the technology sector, noting a shift from semiconductors to software and services since the start of Q3 2026. He attributes this to an "information vacuum" and deleveraging events, but maintains a long-term bullish view on AI adoption, differentiating it from past speculative bubbles. Keller emphasizes the importance of thematic investing in tech and the optionality inherent in hyperscaler CapEx, despite short-term market focus on certainty.

Key discussion points

  • Max Wiethe notes that since the start of Q3 2026, software and services are up 20%, technology hardware and equipment are flat, and semiconductors and semiconductor equipment are down 7.5%.
  • Jeff Keller attributes recent tech sector volatility to an "extreme dislocation" in Q1 2026 where the war in Iran distracted from AI adoption, followed by a Q2 "takeoff moment" with 100% stock gains and heavy retail involvement, and subsequent deleveraging in Korea.
  • Jeff Keller believes that the "real AI winners are CrowdStrike and some like that, rather than maybe some of the semis," and that cyber and infrastructure names have seen a significant bounce due to short-term certainty and usage-based models.
  • Jeff Keller suggests that "the low multiple names across the AI trade in general are going to do better," even if they are at different cycles of earnings growth, because "a lot of the semis are not extrapolating as much as some of the kind of more creative, whether it's behind the meter, some services, highly cyclical businesses, kind of hidden AI winners that people have piled into."
  • Jeff Keller views hyperscaler CapEx as an "optionality thing," a "defensive option" to avoid business wipeout and an "offensive option" for new product generation, even if the near-term ROIC is low.
  • Jeff Keller states that "whenever you get heavy retail involvement in any trade, that usually, in my experience, has meant that the clock is ticking for the end of that trade."
  • Jeff Keller believes that the "sobriety" in the market's reaction to AI announcements, with less dramatic stock movements, is "encouraging" and "better to have a longer, slower cycle."
  • Jeff Keller expresses concern about the "disconnect between the financial analysis of this and the religious analysis that maybe comes from the West Coast" regarding AI build-out, but believes that Silicon Valley leaders will continue spending due to the existential nature of the AI race.
  • Jeff Keller thinks the "profile of return that people associate with these companies [Mag7] is probably behind us," noting increased competition in the hyperscaler market from three vendors to "probably nine or 10 scaled providers."
  • Jeff Keller is "looking at things that are pricing and deceleration and data center starts" as a contrarian opportunity, believing that "they're going to get built" despite political headwinds.
  • Jeff Keller describes his fund as "long biased," with "more than 50% net exposure," and believes the "old school, longshore model is probably in for a renaissance" due to market inefficiencies created by short-term, factor-balanced money.
  • Jeff Keller identifies three key factors for the AI trade: "ARR of the labs and open source," "CapEx," and "the forward prices of compute," all of which he currently sees as "broadly strong."
  • Jeff Keller anticipates that the public listing of AI labs like Anthropic will provide "hugely helpful" metrics, reducing reliance on "guessing and trying to hear third hand" information.
  • Jeff Keller believes that AI tools, while good for facts, can create an "echo chamber" and "more behavioral inefficiency" in markets, leading to "faster narrative cycles" and "more crowded narrative cycles."
