Passive Money Keeps Coming AI Stocks Keep Falling Is Safety the Bubble

Host: Jack Forehand, Matt Zeigler · Guest: Mike Green, Ben Hunt, Peter Bookvar, Robert Hagstrom, Jim Pollson, Mike Hunstead, Cory Host, Adam Butler · Listen at source ↗

Overview

This episode of Excess Returns Weekly Rap features multiple guests discussing current market dynamics. Mike Green and Jim Pollson address the impact of passive investing and the increasing tech exposure in portfolios. Peter Bookvar and Mike Hunstead offer contrasting views on the Federal Reserve's policy and whether the current market environment, particularly in AI, constitutes a bubble. Robert Hagstrom highlights potential overvaluation in defensive stocks, while Cory Host and Adam Butler discuss the practical challenges of diversification.

Key discussion points

  • Mike Green argues that passive investing has created "conditions for false demand through the overweighting of low-float securities into the index," turning indices into "monetization vehicles for large scale IPOs." He anticipates at least "one more gasp of this system that defies all expectations" in the AI complex.
  • Ben Hunt explains the concept of a "common knowledge moment," where a collective realization about a political figure or event fundamentally shifts perceptions and policy expectations. He applies this to Donald Trump's perceived loss of "juice" after the election, predicting an end to "capricious weapon[s] of policy" like tariffs and war escalation.
  • Peter Bookvar believes the Federal Reserve is in a "tweaking cycle" rather than a traditional hiking cycle. He predicts the Fed will not hike in December if the AI trade "continues to roll over" or if Anthropic's IPO valuation is "below the 1.5 trillion which looks like it's now like down to 1.2ish."
  • Robert Hagstrom suggests that "safety is the bubble," arguing that defensive stocks like Walmart, Proctor and Gamble, Colgate, and Costco are trading at "extremely rich relative to their fundamental value" due to a rotation into low-volatility stocks during market sell-offs. He contrasts Walmart's 46x earnings multiple for 5% growth with Nvidia's 19x for 10% growth (prior to Jensen news).
  • Jim Pollson illustrates how investors' allocation to tech has significantly increased over the last 10 years, even without active buying, due to the outperformance of "new era stocks" (tech) relative to "old era stocks." He notes that tech's share of the S&P 500 market cap went from "a little over 20%" 10 years ago to "50/50" recently.
  • Mike Hunstead from Northern Trust dismisses comparisons between the current AI market and the late 1990s dot-com bubble, citing strong fundamentals, profitability, and free cash flow generation of today's leading tech companies. He notes that "equity returns were almost all earnings generated not multiple expansion" in the last year, and forward multiples are "around the 10-year average."
  • Cory Host and Adam Butler discuss the behavioral challenges of diversification, particularly with alternative strategies. They highlight that while a diversified portfolio may be "behaviorally optimal" on paper with "lower drawdowns," clients often struggle to stick with it when "their friends' portfolio goes up 20 or 25%" and their diversified portfolio goes up 6%.
  • Jack Forehand and Matt Zeigler emphasize the importance of rebalancing portfolios to manage unintended exposures that "creep up over time," especially in rapidly growing sectors like AI.

Market & macro takeaways

  • Mike Green views passive investing as distorting demand for low-float securities and facilitating large IPOs, leading to a potential "relief rally in the AI complex" as liquidity is brought to the space.
  • Ben Hunt suggests that a "common knowledge moment" regarding political leadership can lead to a shift in policy, specifically predicting an end to "capricious" policies like tariffs and war escalation.
  • Peter Bookvar believes the Fed is in a "tweaking cycle," not a traditional hiking cycle, and will be sensitive to the performance of the AI trade when making interest rate decisions.
  • Robert Hagstrom argues that defensive stocks (consumer staples) are currently overvalued due to a flight to safety, suggesting a potential "reverse trade" to sell staples and buy tech.
  • Jim Pollson highlights that investors may have significantly higher tech exposure than they realize due to market trends, emphasizing the need for rebalancing.
  • Mike Hunstead asserts that the current AI market is fundamentally different from the dot-com bubble of the 1990s, citing strong earnings and reasonable forward multiples for leading tech companies.
  • Cory Host and Adam Butler stress that while diversified portfolios are optimal on paper, behavioral factors often make them difficult for clients to stick with during periods of concentrated market outperformance. They advocate for "return stacking" to minimize behavioral costs while achieving diversification, using leverage intelligently to add uncorrelated streams without increasing risk.
