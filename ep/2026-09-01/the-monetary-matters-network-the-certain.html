The Certainty Premium Whats Driving the Tech Sector Divergence Jeff Keller

Host: Max Wiethe · Guest: Jeff Keller, Founder and Portfolio Manager of Cape Light Partners · Listen at source ↗

Overview

Jeff Keller discusses the current state of the technology sector, highlighting a significant divergence since Q3 2026, with software and services up 20% while semiconductors are down 7.5%. He argues that long-term uncertainty is driving a premium for short-term certainty in the market, leading to elevated valuations in areas like cyber and infrastructure. Keller believes the AI mega-trend is still in its early stages, despite recent volatility, and that the "religious analysis" from the West Coast regarding AI's existential importance is driving continued investment despite financial concerns.

Key discussion points

  • Jeff Keller notes a "massive divergence" in the tech sector since Q3 2026, with software and services up 20%, technology hardware and equipment flat, and semiconductors and semiconductor equipment down 7.5%.
  • Keller attributes the current market dynamic to high long-term uncertainty, causing anything with short-term certainty to trade at a "huge premium," particularly in cyber and infrastructure.
  • He suggests that application software is still under question, recalibrating from premium valuations to low double-digit or high single-digit growth, making revenue multiples and non-GAAP EPS harder to justify.
  • Keller believes that "the low multiple names across the AI trade in general are going to do better," even if they are at different cycles of earnings growth, as they are not extrapolating as much as some "hidden AI winners."
  • He views the massive CapEx by hyperscalers and AI labs as an "existential" and "defensive option" rather than purely an ROI-driven investment, necessary to avoid being wiped out and to capture open-ended upside.
  • Keller observes a "lot more sobriety in the trade" compared to 2021, with lower market reactions to announcements, which he finds "encouraging" for a longer, slower compounding cycle.
  • He cautions that "whenever you get heavy retail involvement in any trade, that usually, in my experience, has meant that the clock is ticking for the end of that trade," though he doesn't believe the AI trade is over due to its open-ended growth story.
  • Keller identifies three key factors to watch for the AI trade: lab ARRs (adoption), CapEx, and the price of compute, all of which he currently sees as strong.
  • He expresses concern about the "AI tools that are available to a lot of investors and the echo chamber," believing AI will lead to "more behavioral inefficiency" and "overconfidence" rather than removing fear and greed from markets.
  • Keller maintains a "long biased" fund model, with more than 50% net exposure, believing that "the kind of old school longshore model is probably in for a renaissance" due to market inefficiencies created by factor-balanced and short-term focused money.
  • He states it's "unlikely I would ever get net short" on tech, as the confluence of factors seen in late 2021 (government support, retail involvement, cyclicality) rarely exists.
  • Keller emphasizes the importance of "optionality" in portfolio management, maintaining cash or lower gross exposure to be able to step in during "three standard deviation events" that seem to be happening more frequently.
