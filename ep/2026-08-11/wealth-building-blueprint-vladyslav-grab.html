Overview

Mike McGlone argues that the US stock market is at extreme valuation levels, comparable to 1929 and 2008, and is the primary driver of current inflation. He suggests that the market is nearing an endgame where either it corrects, allowing the Fed to ease, or it continues to advance, forcing the Fed to tighten. McGlone expresses concern about non-income producing assets like gold and Bitcoin due to rising US Treasury yields, and predicts lower crude oil prices.

Key discussion points

  • Mike McGlone notes that the US stock market's total market cap is running at almost $82 trillion, which is over two times the public debt of $40 trillion, the highest ratio since 2007 on a year-end basis, and the highest since 2000 if the year ends at current levels.
  • McGlone highlights that the market cap to GDP ratio is running at 2.5 times on a year-end basis, the highest since 2008, indicating extreme valuations.
  • McGlone points out that the US 30-year Treasury yield at 4.21% on a year-end basis is the highest since 2001 (25 years), acting as a major pressure factor for non-producing assets, particularly gold and Bitcoin.
  • McGlone states that he was bullish on gold for decades but is "off that horse" this year, citing its extreme level versus its 10-year moving average and its highest level versus a basket of US Treasuries in 40 years, facing headwinds from 5% treasuries.
  • McGlone expects crude oil to continue its multi-decade trend of making lower highs and lower lows, with lows around $40 a barrel, driven by US supply surplus and declining Chinese demand for petroleum due to EV adoption.
  • McGlone compares the current Bloomberg Commodity Index trajectory to 2008, where it peaked up 30% and ended down 30%, and the S&P 500's current pace to 1929, where it peaked up 30% and ended down 12%.
  • McGlone suggests that the only place left for alpha is in bonds, specifically the US Treasury long bond, which he views as being at a 25-year extreme low in price and extreme valuation versus gold.
  • McGlone believes that if the stock market drops, it could alleviate inflation problems, allowing the Fed to consider easing, potentially leading to a "trade of a lifetime."
