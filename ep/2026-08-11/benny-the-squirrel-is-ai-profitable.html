Is AI Profitable

Host: Rupert Mitchell, Ben Brey · Solo episode · Listen at source ↗

Overview

Rupert Mitchell and Ben Brey discuss the economic underwriting of the hyperscaler capex boom, which they argue is largely predicated on the spending and future valuations of two private AI companies: Anthropic and OpenAI. They present a valuation model for these companies, highlighting the challenges of profitability in a rapidly deflating and competitive AI market, and question the sustainability of current private valuations given the "perishability" of compute and the unique economic dynamics of AI.

Key discussion points

  • Rupert Mitchell argues that the economic underwriting of the hyperscaler capex boom is built on the spending of Anthropic and OpenAI, making their future valuations critical for the entire AI value chain.
  • Ben Brey highlights the rapid pace of change and intense competition in the AI market, noting three ongoing "wars": US vs. China, local vs. cloud models, and intra-cloud competition.
  • Rupert Mitchell's valuation model for Anthropic and OpenAI, which will be available in the show notes, suggests that OpenAI's last private round valuation is approximately 25% above his probability-weighted expected value, and Anthropic's Series H valuation is 8-10% above his estimate. He notes that these valuations already bake in "the best of all possible worlds."
  • Ben Brey introduces the concept of "token economics," comparing LLMs to utilities where electricity generates tokens, capital is chips and cooling, and token price is the utility charge. He emphasizes that the marginal cost of a new user is not near zero, unlike traditional SaaS, due to inference costs.
  • Rupert Mitchell and Ben Brey discuss the "perishability" of compute capacity, similar to power generation, where unused capacity is lost. They argue that engineers will continue to optimize usage, leading to efficiency gains that challenge the profitability of AI models.
  • Ben Brey points out that the "EBTIT" (earnings before training, interest, and tax) metric, used by some AI proponents, is misleading because training is an ongoing OPEX, not a one-off CAPEX, and is crucial for staying competitive.
  • Rupert Mitchell and Ben Brey express skepticism about LLMs as a path to AGI, arguing that even if AGI were achieved, there are limits to the share of economic growth it could take due to human-gated tasks and the cost of energy/compute.
  • Ben Brey notes that enterprise clients are unlikely to repeat past mistakes of data lock-in, suggesting a preference for self-hosted or open-source models over sharing proprietary data with foundation labs, potentially commoditizing the models.

Market & macro takeaways

  • Rupert Mitchell views the current private valuations of OpenAI and Anthropic as potentially overvalued, with "the best of all possible worlds" already priced in, suggesting a lack of IPO discount.
  • Ben Brey highlights the deflationary pressure on AI model pricing due to intense competition (e.g., DeepSeek vs. OpenAI, Anthropic) and the increasing efficiency of users.
  • Rupert Mitchell and Ben Brey suggest that the AI market faces a "recipe for capital misallocation and some capital destruction" due to heavy capital investment, price wars, international competition, and inter-cloud competition.
  • Ben Brey notes that Apple's 20% price increase on its new iPhone could be justified by the inclusion of a local, high-performing AI model, potentially offsetting the cost of cloud-based subscriptions.
  • Rupert Mitchell and Ben Brey argue that the enterprise AI business risks becoming a "consulting type business" (like McKinsey) due to the need for data security and reluctance to share data with foundation labs, limiting scalability.
