Global Liquidity Cycle Turning (1 show)

THESIS: The global liquidity cycle, the primary driver of risk assets, has peaked and is now entering a more dangerous phase. This shift, combined with an ever-growing mountain of debt that needs refinancing, sets the stage for increasing market volatility and a potential major financial crisis within the next few years.

THE THEORY (Michael Howell) Michael Howell — Excess Returns explains that the growth rate of global liquidity peaked in late 2025 and is now slowing. He identifies four phases of the cycle: Rebound, Calm, Speculation, and Turbulence. The market is now on the cusp of the "Turbulence" phase, where risk assets struggle and defensive assets outperform. This cycle is fundamentally a debt refinancing cycle, driven by the average 5-6 year maturity of global debt.

THE IMPLICATIONS (Michael Howell) As liquidity is drawn from financial markets to the real economy, assets like the Russell 2000, Bitcoin, and gold have begun to underperform. The key metric to watch is the debt-to-liquidity ratio; current levels suggest another major financial crisis is likely in the next 2-3 years. The inevitable policy response will be another massive liquidity injection. This outlook makes long-dated government bonds attractive during the turbulence phase, and makes monetary inflation hedges like gold and Bitcoin compelling long-term holds for the subsequent reflation.

KEY TENSION: Whether central banks can navigate the "debt maturity wall" and slowing liquidity without triggering a systemic crisis, or if another crisis-and-bailout cycle is inevitable.

SPOTLIGHT: China's critical minerals off-switch isn't as ironclad as Washington thinks

Host: Andrew Polk, Corey Combs Guest: Jessica DeCarlo, University of Utah

  • Jessica DeCarlo argues that the narrative of Beijing having an "on-off switch" for critical minerals is oversimplified. China's domestic governance is characterized by "fractured extraction," where central ambitions are contested by local governments and firms, limiting absolute control.
  • The concept of "midstream geopolitics" is crucial: China's power lies in its dominance of mineral processing, a strategic chokepoint that requires specialized expertise and long-term investment, which is difficult for the West to replicate quickly.
  • China's strength is also a vulnerability; its dependence on raw material imports for its processing industry creates restraints on its ability to aggressively use export controls without harming its own interests.
  • Western industrial policy often fails because it lacks the integrated, demand-side management of China's approach, which provides guaranteed offtake for new projects, reducing investment risk.
  • DeCarlo proposes "just shoring" as a framework for the West to build resilient supply chains that are socially and environmentally acceptable, integrating these concerns from the start rather than treating them as obstacles.
