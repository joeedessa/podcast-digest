DOMINANT THEMES

The AI Trade at a Crossroads (7 shows)

THESIS: The recent sharp sell-off in technology and semiconductor stocks has forced a debate about the nature of the AI investment theme. One camp views this as a healthy, necessary correction within a powerful, long-term structural bull market. The other sees it as the first tremor of a systemic crash, warning of extreme market concentration, unsustainable valuations, and questionable financial stability within the AI ecosystem.

CORRECTION, NOT CRASH (Dario Perkins, Freya Beamish, Ted Mortensen, Jay Hatfield) This group argues the recent downturn is a "taster" or a "speed check," not the main event. Dario Perkins — GlobalData TS Lombard believes the full AI crash is not due until early 2028, and the current volatility is a healthy process of market discipline preventing a larger bubble. Ted Mortensen — Excess Returns forcefully differentiates this cycle from 1999, arguing AI is a more powerful, all-encompassing technology with a build-out lasting until 2030. Jay Hatfield — Excess Returns points to surging earnings estimates, which have offset higher rates and continue to justify high valuations and drive the market higher.

SYSTEMIC RISK & CONCENTRATION (Steve Eisman, Kai Wu, Rupert Mitchell, Ben Brey) This camp highlights the fragility and risks embedded in the AI trade. Steve Eisman — Steve Eisman is concerned about the financial viability of "neo clouds" like CoreWeave, whose debt yields make building new data centers unfeasible. Kai Wu — Excess Returns quantifies the concentration risk, noting that nearly 50% of the S&P 500 is directly tied to the AI infrastructure trade. Rupert Mitchell and Ben Brey — Benny & The Squirrel point to massive equity raises from hyperscalers as a sign of funding stress and a fundamental shift in market supply dynamics that is "messing with the equilibrium."

KEY TENSION: The resolution depends on whether the AI sector's earnings growth can continue to accelerate, validating current valuations and funding models, even as broader liquidity conditions tighten.

The Global Liquidity Squeeze (3 shows)

THESIS: A contraction in global liquidity is underway, signaling a shift to a more defensive and volatile market environment. This downturn is driven by the immense capital consumption of the AI build-out and a looming "debt maturity wall," which together are draining liquidity from financial markets. This macro headwind presents a significant challenge for risk assets.

LIQUIDITY IS DRAINING (Michael Howell, Chris Casey) Michael Howell — The Monetary Matters Network provides the core thesis, arguing the global liquidity cycle peaked in late 2025 and has entered a "turbulence" phase. He notes that transformative technologies like AI are huge consumers of capital and typically end in recessions. This, combined with a 2026-2030 debt maturity wall, creates immense refinancing pressure. Chris Casey — Wealthion echoes the concern from a US-centric perspective, warning the bond market is "slowly waking up" to the existential threat of US government debt, which he predicts will drive 10-year Treasury yields to 7% by 2028 and necessitates holding inflation hedges.

CENTRAL BANKS ARE MANAGING, NOT TIGHTENING (Michael Howell) While liquidity is draining, Michael Howell — The Monetary Matters Network argues central banks "don't want to tighten" aggressively. He points to the Fed's RMP program and Treasury buybacks as a "new monetary medicine" designed to manage money market liquidity and reduce bond market volatility, ensuring the system remains stable. He also highlights "Treasury QE"—issuing more short-term bills—as a key liquidity impulse into the real economy, creating a "seesaw effect" where the real economy's strength crowds out financial market liquidity.

KEY TENSION: The ability of central banks to subtly manage liquidity and prevent a systemic refinancing crisis will be tested against the sheer scale of the debt maturity wall and the capital demands of the AI sector.

SPOTLIGHT: Tungsten's New Reality: Two Markets, Two Very Different Prices

Host: Trevor Hall Guest: Oliver Friesen, CEO, Guardian Metal Resources

  • The global tungsten market has bifurcated into two distinct markets: a discounted Chinese domestic market and a high-priced Western market.
  • This separation was caused by a Chinese export ban enacted in February of last year and will be further solidified by a DEFARS import restriction on defense procurement from China starting January 1, 2027.
  • Western tungsten prices are materially higher (currently ~$270,000/ton vs. a historic norm below $30,000/ton) due to physical shortages and a geopolitical security premium.
  • Friesen argues this two-market structure is sustainable and necessary to incentivize new Western mine supply to reduce critical mineral dependence on China.
  • Long-term demand drivers include defense applications (munitions) in the US and NATO, and the potential for nuclear fusion technology, which could require tens of thousands of tons of tungsten per reactor.
