MACRO ROOM TEMPERATURE

The market is being driven by an oil supply shock of historic proportions, with the closure of the Strait of Hormuz creating what Rory Johnston — Milk Road Macro calls an "air pocket" in global supply. The central tension is whether this unprecedented physical disruption is priced in. Johnston and Harry Melandri — Maggie Lake Talking Markets argue forcefully that it is not, warning of severe economic consequences and demand destruction. In contrast, voices like Mike Wilson — Morgan Stanley suggest the market has already discounted geopolitical risks and is more sensitive to a potential central bank policy error, creating a stark divide between those focused on the physical economy and those watching financial conditions.

DOMINANT THEME(S)

The Oil Shock & Geopolitical Precipice (7 shows)

The scale of the ongoing oil supply disruption is the day's defining narrative. Rory Johnston — Milk Road Macro provides the most granular and alarming assessment, framing the 20 million barrel per day (bpd) disruption from the Strait of Hormuz closure as the "largest supply shock in the history of the oil market." After accounting for temporary offsets like SPR releases and pipeline rerouting, he calculates a net loss of 6 million bpd that can only be resolved through demand destruction. Harry Melandri — Maggie Lake Talking Markets concurs, arguing the market is severely underpricing the risk of a sustained, existential conflict that could cripple Gulf infrastructure and cause "discontinuous suffering" for energy-dependent economies in Europe and Asia. This view is echoed by Bill Holter — Wealth Building Blueprint Vladyslav Grab, who believes the truth about the conflict is being withheld and that Iranian control of the strait would be disastrous. The market's reaction is seen as a tug-of-war between this physical reality and sentiment. Johnston notes that White House "jawboning" has successfully convinced traders the conflict will be short-lived, keeping prices artificially low. This sets up a potential repricing event as physical scarcity begins to bite in Western markets. The broader market implication was stated most simply by Josh Brown — The Compound: "If oil doesn't go down, the market won't go up — period."

The "Great Migration" to Hard Assets & Stagflation Fears (5 shows)

In response to geopolitical turmoil and persistent inflation, a significant capital rotation is underway. Larry McDonald — The Julia La Roche Show describes this as a "great migration" of capital out of financial assets and into hard assets like oil, gas, copper, silver, and gold. This shift is a direct consequence of the breakdown of the traditional 60/40 portfolio, where stocks and bonds are now selling off in tandem during periods of stress. Aahan Menon — Excess Returns reinforces this, arguing that commodities, especially energy, currently offer the most attractive expected returns, while equities are the least attractive. He advocates for systematic trend-following strategies and avoiding Treasuries until inflation meaningfully subsides. The move into precious metals is a key component of this theme. Bill Holter — Wealth Building Blueprint Vladyslav Grab makes a forceful case for holding physical gold and silver as the ultimate store of value against what he sees as inevitable currency debasement and hyperinflation. While Holter sees new highs by year-end, Jeff deGraaf — RenMac offers a more tactical view, noting that gold's excess alpha suggests it is just beginning to enter a bubble phase, warranting a systematic dollar-cost selling strategy rather than chasing the rally.

Cracks in Private Credit Emerge as a Systemic Risk (3 shows)

Beyond the immediate energy crisis, a consensus is forming around the significant risks embedded in the private credit market. Larry McDonald — The Julia La Roche Show delivers the starkest warning, labeling private credit "this cycle's subprime" and predicting a credit crisis, though he believes it will not cause contagion in public bond markets. His concern is echoed by other seasoned observers. Ed Yardeni — Adam Taggart Thoughtful Money identifies "cracks" in the market, particularly the involvement of retail investors in illiquid assets, as a potential trigger for a recession. Harry Melandri — Maggie Lake Talking Markets also sees stress, pointing to fund gating and withdrawal limits as reminiscent of 2008, and warns that covenant-lite loans issued by shadow banks will cause significant problems during restructuring. The collective view is that this opaque, multi-trillion-dollar market represents a major vulnerability for the financial system.

DIVERGENT VIEWS

Overall Market Outlook

Mike Wilson — Morgan Stanley: Correction, not a bear market. Reasoning: The market has already priced in significant risks, including geopolitical tensions, oil prices, and AI. Over half of Russell 3000 stocks are down at least 20%, and the S&P 500's P/E multiple is down 17%, indicating a well-advanced correction. The primary risk now is a monetary policy mistake, and a Fed pivot could resume the bull market faster than expected. The risk-reward for the next 6-12 months looks better now than at the start of the year.

Jason — Markets Unscripted: Extremely bearish. Reasoning: The market is trading "horribly," with a significant negative character change where previously resilient stocks are breaking down and positive news fails to spark rallies. He cites declining liquidity, widespread and overly complacent bullish sentiment, and the breakdown of market leaders like MU as reasons for his most bearish stance since late 2021.

Energy Stock Positioning

Nick Kolas — The Compound: Hold / Overweight. Reasoning: Energy stocks are the only effective hedge against oil price spikes. Investors should never sell them in this environment and should be at least at an index weight of 4-5%. The sector has performed well as oil has risen and should be held as long as prices are making new highs.

Bill Holter — Wealth Building Blueprint Vladyslav Grab: Avoid. Reasoning: While commodity prices will rise, the uncertainty surrounding the geopolitical conflict is too high to invest in oil and energy equities. He advises converting fiat currency into physical, tangible assets like gold and silver, which cannot default, rather than taking on the equity risk in a highly volatile sector.

SPOTLIGHT: Brixton Continues to Drill High-Grade Silver at Langis

Host: Trevor Guest: Gary Thompson, CEO, Brixton Metals

  • Brixton Metals (BBB, BBBXF) reported "remarkable" new drill results from its Langis silver project in Ontario, including a half-meter interval of 82,334 g/t silver.
  • CEO Gary Thompson described the results as the best hole the company has ever drilled and noted the core was "sparkling with native silver."
  • The company is repositioning exploration capital towards Langis due to its high-grade potential, low all-in drilling costs of around $160/meter, and the strong silver price.
  • The strategy is to drill 60,000 to 80,000 meters over the next 12 months to define a resource by exploring above and below historical workings.
  • Thompson used the phrase "When the ducks are quacking, feed them" to describe the decision to aggressively fund the promising Langis project.

SPOTLIGHT: Is Blue Moon Metals the Best Asymmetric Bet in Mining?

Host: Trevor Hall Guest: Christian Cargle Samard, CEO, Blue Moon Metals

  • Blue Moon Metals (M.V, BMM) is transforming into a critical minerals producer focused on Western supply chains, with a goal of building four mines.
  • The company is employing a "hub and spoke" strategy centered on its permitted Springer Mill complex in Nevada to process ore from its mines in California (Blue Moon) and Utah (Apex).
  • The Apex mine can supply 100% of US domestic germanium needs and 50-100% of gallium needs, while Springer addresses the lack of domestic tungsten production.
  • CEO Christian Cargle Samard sees a significant market disconnect, projecting a potential market cap of C$3.5 billion versus the current C$700 million as the market properly values its assets.
  • He believes analysts are undervaluing the Apex and Springer Tungsten assets, which could each generate over $200 million in annual EBITDA at current prices.

SPOTLIGHT: Lessons from Building 6 Gold Mines

Host: Mat Glisson, Paul Atherley Guest: Matt Wilcox

  • Matt Wilcox, who has built six mines in 14 years, primarily in West Africa, argues the region is often the easiest place to build a mine due to established systems and labor pools.
  • He advocates for an in-house execution strategy for construction, estimating it saves 20-25% on development CAPEX compared to using EPCMs.
  • Wilcox explains the "oxide holiday" phenomenon, where mining soft, oxidized saprolite rock at the start of a mine's life significantly eases operations and accelerates cash flow.
  • He believes West Africa remains chronically under-drilled and that investment in exploration there generally yields discoveries, especially at current gold prices.
  • Wilcox is disinclined to hedge gold, believing investors buy gold equities for direct exposure to the metal's volatility.

SPOTLIGHT: Denison Mines Update

Host: Jimmy Connor Guest: David Cates, CEO & Geoff Smith, VP Corporate Development, Denison Mines

  • Denison Mines has received final permits for its Phoenix ISR mine, the first new Canadian uranium mine approved for construction in over 20 years.
  • Construction began in March for a two-year schedule, targeting first production by mid-2028 with an updated CapEx of $600 million.

  • The company plans to use cash flow from Phoenix to fund the development of its nearby Griffin project, aiming for a combined mine life of 16-18 years.

SPOTLIGHT: Why NASA Hired a Chief Economist

Host: Joe Weisenthal, Tracy Alloway Guest: Alex McDonald, Senior Associate, CSIS

  • NASA's first chief economist role was created to provide independent analysis on market trends and private investment as the agency shifted towards public-private partnerships.
  • The shift was driven by a flat NASA budget since 1972, which required leveraging commercial capabilities to achieve ambitious goals like returning to the moon.
  • Ambitious space programs create significant economic "spinoff effects"; the Apollo program, for example, drove 75% of global semiconductor demand for three years in the 1960s.
  • A nascent low-Earth orbit economy is emerging, with a significant contractual competition underway for a NASA-funded private space station.
  • While private capital is dominant in areas with established markets like launch vehicles, public funding remains crucial for endeavors without viable private markets, such as operating a moon base.
