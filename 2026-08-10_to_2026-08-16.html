WEEK IN REVIEW

A fierce debate over the AI supercycle's viability defined the week, pitting proponents of a durable, multi-year productivity boom against a growing chorus of skeptics warning of a speculative bubble built on fragile foundations. This core tension, voiced by bulls like Dom Rizzle and bears like Ed Zitron and Steve Eisman, overshadowed a deteriorating bond market and a Federal Reserve trapped between funding a hot economy and preventing a credit event. By week's end, the market remained split between following AI-fueled momentum and rotating into hard assets as a hedge against a new, more inflationary economic regime.

NARRATIVE TRACKER

The AI Supercycle: Productivity Boom or Speculative Bubble? — DOMINANT The week's central narrative evolved from a general concern over capital misallocation (Rich Bernstein, David Rosenberg) into a direct confrontation between two camps. The bulls, led by Dom Rizzle and Paul Krake, argued the AI buildout is a durable, multi-year investment cycle justified by strong ROICs and productivity gains, viewing it as a "golden age for equity investing." The skeptics grew louder and more specific as the week progressed. Ed Zitron claimed the boom was fueled by circular financing from unprofitable startups, while Luke Gromen called it "fiscally problematic." The narrative culminated with Steve Eisman's warning of an "Achilles' heel": the entire ecosystem's dependency on just two unproven LLMs, OpenAI and Anthropic, creating a systemic risk to the market's primary engine.

The Fed's Policy Trap & Bond Market Fragility — DOMINANT The Federal Reserve's credibility was a live issue all week. The narrative was framed by two opposing forces. One camp, led by Darius Dale and Michael Howell, argued a rising neutral rate (R*) and a robust real economy are pushing long-term yields structurally higher, forcing the Fed to tighten further to avoid a bond market crisis. The opposing view, articulated forcefully by Jim Paulsen, was that a "growth scare" is imminent, evidenced by weakening labor and income data, which will force the Fed into a dovish pivot and rate cuts before year-end. The US CPI release mid-week failed to resolve this tension, leaving the market caught between a hawkish reality and dovish expectations.

The Hard Asset Imperative — BUILDING What began Monday as a high-conviction tactical trade in precious metals (Mike Preston) broadened into a strategic, regime-change thesis by the weekend. The rationale expanded from a simple inflation hedge to a necessary response to a new economic order. Andy Schectman and Jim Rickards pointed to massive, underreported central bank buying as a de-dollarization signal. Michael Howell identified China's internal liquidity injections as the primary driver for gold. By Saturday, Luke Gromen framed the entire shift as a move from neoliberalism to "Hamiltonian economics," where reshoring and secular inflation make holding hard assets and avoiding long-duration bonds imperative.
